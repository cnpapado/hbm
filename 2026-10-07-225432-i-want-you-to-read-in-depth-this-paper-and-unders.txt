⏺ Short answer: no. HAL (the MIT paper) does physical place-and-route of a
  single qLDPC code onto chip hardware. It works at the level of physical
  qubits, couplers, wires, bump bonds and TSVs, the same level as VLSI/EDA
  design. DASCOT compiles a logical circuit onto an array of logical qubits.
  Both papers use the words "mapping/placing" and "routing", but they mean
  different things at different layers of the stack.

  What the HAL paper actually does

  Problem. qLDPC codes (BB/gross, tile, radial, Tanner, directional) need
  non-local stabilizers. The paper asks whether you can build the physical
  couplers needed to measure every stabilizer directly in multilayer
  superconducting hardware, and how hard that is to fabricate.

  Input. The code's connectivity (Tanner) graph, read from the parity-check
  matrix. Nodes are physical data and check qubits; edges are the physical
  couplers that syndrome extraction needs. Check and data qubits are treated the
  same, and the X/Z basis is ignored.

  Hardware model.
  - A stack of flip-chip "tiers". Tier 0 holds the qubits.
  - Each higher tier is another bump-bonded chip with two opposing routing
    layers.
  - A coupler can switch layers within a tier through a bump bond, and climb to
    a higher tier through a TSV.

  Algorithm.
  1. Planar subgraph. Louvain community detection gives a rough layout. Edges
     are sorted (short intra-community edges first, then inter-community edges)
     and added greedily while a Hopcroft–Tarjan planarity test still passes.
  2. Placement. A Kamada–Kawai spring layout of the planar subgraph is snapped
     to a grid (round to the nearest point, then resolve collisions with a
     min-heap) and compacted. For BB and tile codes the user can supply
     positions instead, e.g. a square lattice.
  3. Qubit tier. Edges are drawn as straight, crossing-free segments. Failures
     go into a FIFO queue.
  4. Higher tiers. Edges are routed shortest first: a straight line with bump
     transitions at obstructions, falling back to A* on a 3D occupancy grid. An
     edge that needs more than 10 bumps is re-queued. If an edge comes up a
     second time in the same tier, the tier counts as congested and a new tier
     is added.

  Metric. Hardware complexity C_hw = 1 + weighted mean of four normalized
  quantities: number of tiers, average long-edge length, bumps per edge, and
  TSVs per edge.
  - The baseline is a surface code, which scores C_hw = 1.
  - The "optimistic" values are 5 tiers, 10× coupler length, 4 bumps and 3 TSVs;
    a layout at all four scores C_hw = 2.
  - C_hw is plotted against logical efficiency kd²/n, taken from the
    Bravyi–Poulin–Terhal bound.

  Findings.
  - There is a clear tradeoff between logical efficiency and hardware
    complexity.
  - The gross code [[144,12,12]] needs 5 tiers, even though it has "thickness
    2". Allowing layer transitions shortens edges at the cost of extra layers.
    About 2/3 of its edges sit on a nearest-neighbour weight-4 qubit tier.
  - Tile codes have open boundaries. They form flat C_hw bands that don't grow
    with size, and their edges are about 4× shorter than the gross code's (e.g.
    [[188,8,9]] fits in 3 tiers). Their efficiency is lower.
  - BB codes get harder to build as they grow, because of the periodic
    boundaries. For high-aspect-ratio BB codes, the spring layout beats the
    square grid by up to 4×.
  - Weight-4 radial codes look best. However, the authors could not reliably
    find instances that reach distance 2s, so those points use lower-distance
    codes as proxies.
  - Tanner codes are the hardest to build for a given efficiency.

  What the paper does not do:
  - No circuit, no time dimension and no logical gates.
  - No noise or logical-error simulation.
  - No syndrome-extraction scheduling.
  - No frequency allocation or crosstalk modelling.

  Where HAL sits relative to DASCOT. For surface codes, HAL's problem is
  trivial: the code is a 2D nearest-neighbour lattice, so C_hw = 1. DASCOT takes
  that physical layer for granted. HAL solves the layer underneath DASCOT,
  which only becomes hard once you move to qLDPC codes.

  Structural similarities:
  - Both route edges one after another along shortest available paths, and the
    processing order decides the outcome.
  - HAL's tiers play the role of DASCOT's time steps: conflicting edges spill
    into a new tier, just as conflicting gates spill into a new time step.

  HAL uses a fixed shortest-first order with a FIFO queue. That is exactly the
  baseline DASCOT shows to be suboptimal: searching over routing orders improved
  90% of circuits in DASCOT's ablation. Applying DASCOT-style order search, or
  simulated-annealing placement, to HAL is a plausible, cheap improvement.

  What a "DASCOT for qLDPC" would be instead

  It would be a compiler for logical circuits on qLDPC memories. Its problems
  would include:
  - assigning program qubits to logical slots inside code blocks (the gross code
    holds 12 per block);
  - scheduling logical Pauli-product measurements through ancilla, bridge or
    adapter systems between blocks;
  - using in-block automorphisms;
  - serialization within a block;
  - routing from magic-state factories.

  The closest framing among the papers HAL cites is Tour de Gross (Yoder et al.,
  ref. [49]). HAL points toward this only in its outlook, where it proposes
  measuring how hardware complexity rises when an "ancilla graph" is added for
  logical computation. That addition would be the hardware cost of what a qLDPC
  version of DASCOT would then schedule.

⏺ I've now read both papers end to end, including HAL's appendices and figures,
  and checked the paper's numbers myself. The answer is still no. HAL works
  below the logical level: it designs the physical hardware for one code.
  Checking the numbers also turned up some caveats that a quick read misses.

  1. Where each paper sits

  Logical circuit (CNOT/T, Pauli measurements)
          │  DASCOT: place program qubits on logical patches, route 
  lattice-surgery
          │          paths per time step, minimize number of time steps
          ▼
  Logical layout (surface-code patches on a grid)
          │  (surface code: trivial, a 2D nearest-neighbour lattice)
          ▼
  Physical chip (qubits, couplers, wires, bumps, TSVs)
             HAL: place physical data and check qubits, route permanent coupler
                  wires through stacked chips, minimize fabrication complexity

  For surface codes the bottom layer is trivial, so DASCOT can assume it. For
  qLDPC codes the bottom layer is hard, and that is the problem HAL solves. HAL
  has:
  - no circuit, no time steps, no logical gates;
  - no magic states, no logical qubits;
  - no simulation of syndrome extraction.

  Its output is a static chip design plus a score.

  2. HAL in detail

  Input.
  - The Tanner graph read from the parity-check matrix: every data and check
    qubit is a node, and every check-to-data coupler is an edge.
  - The X/Z basis and the order in which checks are scheduled are deliberately
    ignored.

  Hardware it assumes.
  - A stack of flip-chip "tiers". Tier 0 holds the qubits.
  - Each higher tier is an added bump-bonded chip with two facing routing
    layers.
  - A wire can cross another wire by hopping to the facing layer through a bump.
    It reaches a higher tier through a TSV.

  Placement.
  1. Louvain community detection gives an initial layout.
  2. Edges are sorted: short edges inside a community first, then edges between
     communities.
  3. Edges are added greedily if a Hopcroft–Tarjan test says the graph stays
     planar. This builds a large planar subgraph.
  4. A Kamada–Kawai spring layout of that subgraph is snapped to the grid
     (round, then resolve collisions with a min-heap) and compacted.
  5. For BB and tile codes the user can supply positions instead, e.g. a square
     lattice. For tile codes this includes where each check qubit sits; placing
     it to minimize the sum of Euclidean distances to its data qubits worked
     best (App. F).

  Routing.
  1. Tier 0: straight, crossing-free segments only. Edges that fail go into a
     FIFO queue.
  2. Tier n ≥ 1: edges are tried in ascending length. Each is first drawn
     straight, switching layers through a bump at every obstruction. If that
     fails, A* searches a 3D grid with 8 in-plane moves plus a vertical move.
  3. An edge needing more than 10 bumps is re-queued.
  4. If an edge comes up again within the same tier, the tier counts as
     congested and a new tier is added.

  Score. C_hw = 1 + mean of four normalized quantities: tiers, average long-edge
  length, bumps, and TSVs.
  - The baseline is the surface code: 1 tier, length 1, 0 bumps, 0 TSVs.
  - The optimistic values are 5 tiers, 10× length, 4 bumps, 3 TSVs. A layout at
    those values scores C_hw = 2.
  - I recomputed the gross, directional, tile and radial rows from Table V with
    this formula. All four match the published values to two decimals, so I
    understand the metric exactly.

  3. What checking the numbers revealed

  codes.

  every radial code at d = 2s. But the authors' own search (Table IV) often
  found lower distances. I recomputed kd²/n with the distances they actually
  verified:

  - Every weight-4 radial code they actually verified has kd²/n ≤ 2, barely
    above the surface code.
  - So the claim that weight-4 radial codes could beat everything in efficiency
    at low hardware cost depends entirely on the conjecture that distance-2s
    instances exist.
  - Only the weight-8 instances (4,7), (4,11) and (4,13), plus (3,3), (3,5) and
    the tiny (2,2), are verified at d = 2s.
  - Their argument that C_hw doesn't depend on distance for a fixed (r,s) (Table
    IV ratios ≈ 1) is fine. But that only fixes the y-axis, not the x-axis.

  2. Any code with a second tier gets a fixed jump in C_hw.

  - Every edge on a higher tier needs about 2 TSVs, one at each endpoint. So
    once any edge leaves tier 0, the TSV term jumps from 0 to about 0.67, adding
    about 0.17 to C_hw.
  - Add the tier term and the minimum bump count, and even the simplest
    non-planar code lands around 1.4–1.5.
  - The authors note this "non-negligible offset" relative to the surface code.
    Much of it is this step in how the metric is defined, rather than a gradual
    cost.

  3. C_hw scores HAL's heuristic, not the code.

  - HAL lays out the gross code in 5 tiers, but it is known to fit in 2 planar
    layers ("thickness 2", Bravyi et al.).
  - The authors say this is a deliberate tradeoff: shorter wires in exchange for
    more layers. They leave a head-to-head comparison to future work.
  - HAL also isn't optimizing anything globally. It uses fixed greedy orders
    with no search, so its tier count is an upper bound, not a property of the
    code.
  - Comparisons between code families therefore mix up what the code requires
    with how well HAL's heuristics suit that code's structure. The aspect-ratio
    study (App. E) shows this: just switching between the spring layout and the
    square lattice changes C_hw by up to 4×.

  4. Things outside the model. Logical efficiency kd²/n leaves out:
  - threshold, circuit-level distance and decoding;
  - frequency crowding and crosstalk;
  - the capacitance limits of a degree-6–10 qubit;
  - the depth of the syndrome-extraction circuit;
  - any ability to compute, as opposed to just store, logical information.

  C_hw leaves out all of the first four as well.

  4. Where HAL and DASCOT actually line up

  The two papers share an EDA-style "place, then route sequentially" design, and
  the correspondence is quite precise:

  ┌─────────────────┬───────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────┐
  │                 │          HAL          │            DASCOT             │
  ├─────────────────┼───────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────┤
  │ Thing placed    │ Physical qubits →     │ Program qubits → logical      │
  │                 │ grid points           │ patches                       │
  ├─────────────────┼───────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────┤
  │ Thing routed    │ Permanent coupler     │ Temporary ancilla paths for   │
  │                 │ wires                 │ lattice surgery               │
  ├─────────────────┼───────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────┤
  │ Conflict        │ Two wires crossing on │ Two paths sharing a vertex in │
  │                 │  one layer            │  one time step                │
  ├─────────────────┼───────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────┤
  │ How conflict is │ Bump hop, or a new    │ Delay to a new time step      │
  │  resolved       │ tier (space)          │ (time)                        │
  ├─────────────────┼───────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────┤
  │                 │ Fixed: ascending      │ Searched with simulated       │
  │ Routing order   │ length, FIFO          │ annealing, cost = −Σ          │
  │                 │                       │ criticality                   │
  ├─────────────────┼───────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────┤
  │ Placement       │ Implicit (planarity,  │ Explicit (conflicts within a  │
  │ objective       │ spring energy)        │ layer), searched with SA      │
  └─────────────────┴───────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────┘

  HAL's routing order is exactly the shortest-first baseline (EDPC) that
  DASCOT's ablation beats. Searching the order improved 90% of circuits. HAL's
  "tier" plays the same role as DASCOT's "time step": a congested set of edges
  spills into the next one.

  So DASCOT-style techniques could plausibly lower HAL's tier counts:
  - an annealed edge order with a cost that rewards routing edges that block
    many others;
  - an annealed placement scored by predicted crossings.

  That would improve HAL. It would not turn HAL into a logical compiler.

  5. What a DASCOT for qLDPC would have to solve

  The logical level of qLDPC codes is structurally different from surface-code
  patches:
  - Logical qubits are not spatially separate. One block (e.g. the gross code)
    stores k = 12 logical qubits in shared physical qubits. "Mapping" means
    choosing which block and which logical slot holds each program qubit.
  - The operations are different. They are:
    - logical Pauli-product measurements through an ancilla system attached to
      the block;
    - a small set of cheap in-block automorphism gates;
    - bridges between blocks;
    - magic-state injection.
  - Contention is about shared measurement hardware, not crossing paths. Each
    block can perform only a limited number of logical measurements at a time,
    so the binding constraint is exclusivity on that hardware and on inter-block
    links. HAL itself notes that logical computation in qLDPC codes is "often
    serialized", costing about an order of magnitude in time for gross codes.

  Some of DASCOT's ideas carry over, with changes:
  - The layered interaction graph still applies, with a different goal. Program
    qubits that are active in the same dependency layer should go in different
    blocks so they can run in parallel. Qubits that only interact across layers
    can share a block.
  - Criticality-weighted order search maps onto scheduling measurements under
    per-block exclusivity.
  - Vertex-disjoint paths get replaced by resource-exclusivity constraints plus
    the topology of the bridges between blocks.

  To check what already exists, I'd look at Tour de Gross (Yoder et al. 2025,
  HAL's ref. [49]), which sets out a modular gross-code architecture. The
  underlying measurement gadgets are in the work on gauging logical operators,
  improved qLDPC surgery and bridges, and "extractors". As far as I know these
  are architecture and gadget papers rather than optimizing compilers in
  DASCOT's sense. Verify that against the current literature before relying on
  it, because this area moves fast.

  Where the two layers meet. HAL's outlook proposes measuring how much harder a
  code becomes to build when an "ancilla graph" for computation is added. That
  is the natural link:
  - a logical compiler decides which measurement and bridge resources a workload
    needs;
  - HAL-style layout then prices what those resources cost to fabricate.

  Neither paper closes that loop. DASCOT's own future work points the same way:
  heterogeneous multi-chip architectures where cross-chip CNOTs cost more.

⏺ Yes. As of this year there is work that takes .qasm circuits and does
  logical-level mapping for qLDPC codes. All of it targets IBM's gross /
  bivariate-bicycle "bicycle architecture", and none of it yet does the full
  DASCOT job: dependency-aware mapping plus routing that minimizes the number of
  time steps.

  Closest analog to DASCOT

  Sethi, Khan, Poster, Anand & Baker, "Optimizing Logical Mappings for Quantum 
  Low-Density Parity Check Codes" (arXiv:2603.17167, March 2026)

  - Input: QASMBench .qasm files, transpiled to Clifford+T and then to
    Pauli-based computation (a list of Pauli-product rotations).
  - Target: gross-code modules, each holding 12 logical qubits with one slot
    kept as the "pivot", arranged in a line or a grid with T-state factories.
  - What it maps: a two-level problem: which module each program qubit goes
    into, then where each module sits in the topology.
  - How:
    - Stage 1 partitions qubits into ⌈n/11⌉ clusters with KaHyPar. Each
      Pauli-product rotation is a weighted hyperedge.
    - Stage 2 places modules with a priority heuristic: modules used least often
      go farthest from the factory. They prove this is optimal for a line with
      one factory at the end.
  - Objective: errors from inter-module measurements, which they identify as the
    largest error source in the gross architecture. Not time steps.
  - Results: compared with adapted SABRE, METIS, time-slice partitioning and a
    greedy baseline, about 13% lower mapping-dependent error on average and
    about 36% at best.

  How it compares to DASCOT:

  Interaction model
  DASCOT: Layered interaction graph (sensitive to gate order)
  Sethi et al.: Hyperedges weighted by how often qubits interact (ignores gate
  order, the very thing DASCOT criticized in Autobraid)
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Routing
  DASCOT: A time-step schedule with vertex-disjoint paths; the order is searched

  with simulated annealing
  Sethi et al.: A static spanning-tree cost per rotation, with no scheduling
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Parallelism
  DASCOT: Central
  Sethi et al.: Not modelled, because Pauli-based computation runs one rotation
  at a time
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Optimal baseline
  DASCOT: SAT solver
  Sethi et al.: Optimality proof only for a line with one factory
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Code
  DASCOT: Open (Docker artifact)
  Sethi et al.: I found no public repository

  Other relevant work

  Logical-level, takes a circuit, targets qLDPC:
  - Liu, Foxman, Anselmetti & Y. Ding, "Assessing System Capabilities and 
    Bottlenecks of an Early Fault-Tolerant Bicycle Architecture" 
    (arXiv:2604.20013).
    - A full pipeline from PennyLane and MQTBench circuits, through Pauli-based
      computation, to bicycle instructions.
    - Schedules on a weighted DAG with critical-path analysis.
    - Reuses Sethi's allocator, and explicitly does no placement inside a
      module.
    - Key finding: the dominant bottleneck is inter-module communication caused
      by non-Clifford operations, i.e. delivering T states. That bears directly
      on your magic-state bandwidth work.
    - Code is "promised in a few months".
  - IBM bicycle-architecture-compiler (Tour de gross, arXiv:2506.03094).
    - Open source.
    - Takes Pauli-based-computation input or Qiskit circuits and compiles to the
      bicycle instruction set.
    - Does no mapping or routing optimization; it is the baseline the papers
      above improve on.
  - Bhaumik et al., arXiv:2609.00852.
    - Placement and routing of multi-controlled Toffolis on BB modules, using
      the gates' binary-tree structure.
    - Also optimizes where factories go (facility location).
    - Narrow scope.
  - HetEC (Stein et al., ASPLOS 2025).
    - Gross code as memory plus surface code for computation, connected by an
      ancilla bus.
    - A full system study rather than an optimizing mapper.

  Beyond the surface code, but not qLDPC:
  - Herzog, Berent, Kubica & Wille, "Lattice Surgery Compilation beyond the 
    Surface Code" (QCE 2025, arXiv:2504.10591, open source in MQT QECC).
    - Recasts compilation as mapping and routing on a "macroscopic routing
      graph" and minimizes the depth of CNOT+T circuits.
    - Targets the color code and folded surface code, where each patch still
      holds one logical qubit. That makes it structurally the closest to DASCOT,
      but not high-rate.

  Not logical-level (easy to confuse): HAL (the paper you sent), ONEX
  (2608.20164), Park-n-Ride (2608.17023), Louvre, and flag-proxy networks. All
  of these schedule syndrome extraction or lay out physical hardware.

  Gadgets that a qLDPC compiler would target (these are not compilers 
  themselves): bridges/improved surgery (Cross et al.), extractors, gauging
  logical operators, universal adapters, parallel surgery, SSIP, GeneCS.

  The recent survey arXiv:2609.17465 agrees with this picture. It says there is
  "no single corresponding patch-level abstraction" beyond the surface code, and
  lists turning qLDPC advantages into executable compiler stacks as an open
  direction.

  The open gap

  A real "DASCOT for qLDPC" would need things none of these papers do yet:
  - Dependency-aware mapping: keep qubits active in the same layer in different
    modules so they can run in parallel; the current work groups by interaction
    frequency instead.
  - Scheduling time steps across parallel resources: each module's measurement
    unit is a resource, inter-module bridges are routing resources that can
    conflict, and factory output is contended.
  - Beyond one-at-a-time Pauli rotations: the existing work assumes a strictly
    sequential model, which hides the parallelism question entirely.
  - An exact SAT/ILP baseline for small instances.

  Caveats:
  - Most of these are 2026 preprints that have not been peer reviewed.
  - My details come from abstracts and HTML pages I fetched, so check them
    against the PDFs before citing, especially code availability.

  The core result to derive

  Here is a first-order model I derived; you'll need to validate it.

  - Unit of time: one lattice-surgery step, about d cycles.
  - Workload parameters:
    - ρ: T gates per logical qubit per step;
    - β: burstiness, peak load over mean load per block;
    - working set W and locality, for the hierarchy part.
  - Stacked surface code: STV ≈ D · N · 2d² · (1 + 1/S), where D is the logical
    depth.
  - qLDPC blocks:
    - k = logical qubits per block, n = physical qubits per block, α = adapter
      overhead, c = concurrent surgery ports per block, t_op = time per surgery
      operation.
    - STV ≈ D · (N/k) · n(1+α) · max(1, β·k·ρ·t_op / c).
  - Below saturation (βkρt_op < c): qLDPC wins by the full space ratio R,
    compared at matched logical error rate.
  - Above saturation: k cancels out. The comparison becomes physical qubits per
    concurrent magic port:
    - qLDPC wins iff β·ρ·t_op · n(1+α)/c < 2d²(1 + 1/S);
    - equivalently, ρ* ≈ 2d²(1 + 1/S) · c / (β · t_op · n(1+α)).
    - That is the exchange rate the reviewer is asking for.
  - Mapping is the one compiler knob that matters. T-aware assignment of qubits
    to blocks lowers β and moves the crossover. Treat it as a sensitivity axis,
    in the style of Sethi et al.'s partitioning, not as the main contribution.

     ▼
  1. Clean it up (same for everyone)

     ▼
  2. Measure the circuit (N, ρ, β, working set)

  3. Fix the rules (target error, connectivity budget)
     │
     ├──► 4a. 2D surface code   ─┐
     ├──► 4b. HBMS (stacked)    ─┤   each returns:
     ├──► 4c. qLDPC blocks      ─┤   qubits Q, time T, error
     └──► 4d. Hierarchy         ─┘

                                 ▼

     │
     ▼

  1. Clean it up. Run wisq --mode opt to turn foo.qasm into a Clifford+T
  circuit. Every architecture gets this same file, so none gets an easier
  circuit.

  2. Measure the circuit. Run workload_stats.py to get:
  - N: number of qubits;
  - ρ: how many T gates per qubit per step;
  - β: how bursty the T gates are (peak vs average);
  - working set: how many qubits are "active" at once, which the hierarchy
    needs.

  These numbers tell you where the circuit sits on the crossover map.

  3. Fix the rules.
  - Target error: e.g. "the whole program must succeed 99% of the time". That
    sets the surface-code distance d and the qLDPC code (gross or two-gross).
  - Connectivity budget: e.g. "one extra layer". That decides what each design
    is allowed:
    - HBMS: how many magic patches on top (S);
    - qLDPC: how many ports per block (c);
    - hierarchy: how big the compute region is.

  4. Run all four on the same circuit.
  - 2D: wisq/DASCOT with factories on the edge.
  - HBMS: your fork, magic states on top.
  - qLDPC:
    - pack qubits 12 per block;
    - schedule the gates;
    - T gates on the same block wait in line.
  - Hierarchy:
    - qubits live in qLDPC "memory";
    - they move to surface-code "compute" when needed;
    - moving costs time.

  Each one returns three numbers: physical qubits Q, runtime T in cycles, and
  the total error.

  5. Compare.
  - Discard any design that missed the error target.
  - Compute STV = Q × T for the rest. Lowest wins.

  6. Check the model. Feed the step-2 numbers into model.py. If it predicts the
  same winner and roughly the same STV, the model works.

  Then repeat for many .qasm files and parameter sweeps. Each run is one dot on
  the crossover map. The map shows which design wins in which region. That map
  is your design law, and that's the paper.

❯ who should schedule the gates for qldpc?                                      
⏺ You write it yourself. It's a small script, qldpc_sched.py. None of the
  existing tools does this job:
  - IBM bicycle compiler: only runs operations one at a time and doesn't
    schedule in parallel.
  - Sethi et al.: only decides which block each qubit goes in, and the code
    isn't public.
  - Liu/Ding: not released yet.

  What the scheduler does:
  1. Find the gates that are ready, meaning all the gates before them are done.
  2. Sort them by criticality, the same rule DASCOT uses. Gates with long chains
     of gates waiting on them go first.
  3. For each gate, check whether its resources are free:
     - CNOT inside a block: a port on that block.
     - CNOT across blocks: a port on both blocks plus the link between them.
     - T gate: a port on the block plus a magic state from a factory.
  4. If everything is free, start the gate and mark those resources busy for its
     duration (from the Tour de gross table). If not, the gate waits.
  5. Move time forward and repeat until every gate is done.

  That's about 200–400 lines of Python.

  Why a simple scheduler is enough: the effect you're measuring is T gates on
  one block queuing behind a single port. That queuing comes from the hardware
  limit (c ports), not from how clever the scheduler is. Any reasonable
  scheduler will show it.

  - Use the same criticality rule as DASCOT on the surface-code side, so neither
    side gets a smarter scheduler.
  - Run a second, dumber policy (first-come-first-served) and show the crossover

  - Report the ideal lower bound (circuit depth, no waiting) next to every

  What it shows

  1. Runtime grows linearly with T count (fig_runtime_vs_T.png). For T-heavy
     circuits the machine produces exactly one T per factory period: about 234
     cycles per T against a 229-cycle factory + injection latency. Adding
     modules adds no T bandwidth.
  2. One module is ~100% busy while the others sit at 5–30%
     (fig_module_utilization.png). There are two kinds of bottleneck:
     - T-heavy circuits (QFT, Ising, DNN, VQE, factoring): the module next to
       the factory saturates, so the limit is the T-injection port.
     - Arithmetic circuits with few T gates (adders, multipliers, qram): the
       data module saturates. Each multi-qubit Pauli rotation needs 10–20
       in-module measurements, about 1,600–2,600 cycles per T. The limit is the
       module's measurement unit, not the factory.

     Either way, runtime is set by the most congested block, which is exactly
     the reviewer's claim, now measured in IBM's model.
  3. Error decides which code you can use.
     - Gross at p = 1e-3 fails beyond roughly 500 T gates.
     - Gross at p = 1e-4 works up to about 50k T gates; the 7–8M-T circuits
       fail.
     - Two-gross handles those (error ~1e-11), but costs about 20k qubits, of
       which 18.6k is the T factory.
  4. Arbitrary-angle rotations dominate T counts. Each one becomes about 90 T
     gates at the compiler's default synthesis accuracy. For example, qaoa_n6's
     94 rotations become 7,158 T gates.

    sweep.
  - total_error is an additive union bound, so values above 1 mean certain

⏺ It's a valid question, and a reviewer will ask it. But the premise mixes up
  two separate things, and adjacent cultivation is not better on every metric.

  The answer: cultivation is a source, HBMS is a placement

  - Cultivation vs distillation is about how magic states are made.
  - In-plane vs on top is about where the sources sit.

  HBMS is a placement choice, so it can host cultivation. The real comparison is
  adjacent cultivation vs cultivation on top, not "cultivation vs HBMS".

  Where adjacent cultivation loses

  1. Rate.
  - A cultivation patch is slow and random. IBM's cultivation factory takes
    about 350 cycles per state, which is about 15–20 lattice-surgery steps.
  - So one patch per qubit only keeps up below roughly ρ ≈ d / t_cult ≈ 0.05 T
    gates per qubit per step.
  - Above that, each qubit needs several patches. In-plane you can't add them: a
    qubit has at most 4 neighbours, and each extra patch takes a routing tile.
  - On top, there are about 5 free tiles above each data qubit, each one
    vertical hop away.
  - Having several patches per qubit also smooths cultivation's random latency.

  2. Fidelity floor.
  - Cultivation tops out at a limited T-state fidelity, roughly 1e-9 at p =
    1e-3. Check the exact numbers in Gidney et al. 2024.
  - Large algorithms need per-T error well below 1/(T count). Something with
    about 1e9 T gates needs distillation fed by cultivated states.
  - That brings back large factories, and with them delivery, which is the case
    HBMS addresses.

  3. Dense layouts. In compact layouts there's no free neighbour tile for a
  cultivation patch. Adjacent cultivation only works in sparse grids, and sparse
  grids cost area.

  When adjacent cultivation wins

  - low T rate;
  - a fidelity target cultivation can reach;
  - a sparse layout;
  - high vertical-coupling noise p′.

  That's a real regime, and you should say so.

  Check it before running anything

  For each benchmark, compute two numbers:
  1. Required T rate: is ρ greater than d / t_cult?

  patch matters.

  Example: qubit q needs a T gate at timesteps 1, 2, 3 and 4, and each patch
  takes 15 timesteps to recharge.

  1 patch:   T at step 1 ... wait ... T at 16 ... wait ... T at 31 ... T at 46
  2 patches: T at 1, T at 2 ... wait ... T at 16, T at 17

  - It helps when a qubit needs T gates more often than one patch can recharge,

  - It doesn't help when a qubit rarely needs a T gate, because one patch is
    always ready in time.

❯ If a qubit needs T gates more often than one patch can recharge, and there is 
  no available until recharging what happens?                                   

⏺ The T gate waits. It stays in the queue until a patch has a fresh T state, and
  so does every later gate that depends on that qubit. Gates on other qubits
  keep running in the meantime.

  How much the wait hurts depends on how much of the circuit is stuck behind
  that qubit:
  - On the critical path: the whole circuit gets longer.
  - Off the critical path: the wait may hide behind other work and cost nothing.

  What a real machine (or your scheduler) can do instead of waiting:
  1. Wait. This is the simplest option and the honest default.
  2. Borrow a neighbour's patch. If a nearby patch is ready and unused, route to
     it on the top layer. That costs a longer path and may block other routes.
     This is basically what your Shared-k routing already does.
  3. Fall back to a far source, such as an edge factory, at the cost of a long
     route.

  Two facts to model:
  - A ready patch can hold its T state until it's used, so a patch is
    effectively a one-state buffer. Recharging starts only once the state is
    consumed.
  - Cultivation sometimes fails and restarts, so recharge time is random, not
    fixed. Draw it from a distribution, or use the average to start.

  In your code, the change is small:
  - Give each patch a ready_at timestep.
  - When routing a T gate, skip patches with ready_at > now.
  - If no ready patch is reachable, defer the gate to the next timestep, exactly
    like a blocked path.
  - After a T state is used, set ready_at = now + recharge_time.

  Optional knob: the borrow distance, i.e. how many hops a qubit may go to use
  another qubit's patch. With 0 it only ever waits; larger values approach the
  shared configurations.

❯ What is this  Patches per qubit │ 1 / 2 / 4 ?                                 

⏺ It's how many cultivation patches serve each data qubit.

  - 1: one patch per qubit, like your shared_none. While it recharges, that
    qubit's next T gate waits.
  - 2: two patches per qubit. While one recharges, the other is ready, so
    there's less waiting but more qubits.
  - 4: four patches per qubit, one on each side. This is what Gidney 2025 does,
    with the neighbours taking turns.

  Why sweep it: patches recharge slowly. More patches per qubit means fewer
  stalls but more qubits. The sweep finds the best balance for each design and
  each T density.

  Why it's interesting for your comparison:
  - In-plane (B1): at most 4 patches per qubit, and every extra one takes
    routing space or makes the grid grow.
  - On top (P1, P3): extra patches sit above the qubit and don't touch the data
    plane.

  Two notes:
  - It applies only to the cultivation designs (B1, P1, P3). For factory
    designs, the matching knob is how many factories you build.
  - You can also go below 1: one patch shared by 2 or 4 qubits, which is the
    same as your Shared-k. For low T density that saves qubits. So the full
    sweep is 1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4 patches per qubit.

  Short answer: with four patches in the plane, a qubit can't do CNOTs at all.
  Every side is taken, so no route can reach it. That's panel (a).

  Why Gidney does it anyway: in his paper the four-patch setup is a dedicated T 
  station. One patch runs a long sequence of T gates (preparing a phase-gradient
  state) and never needs CNOTs. It's not a layout for general compute qubits.

  The rule that limits it in the plane (DASCOT): a CNOT route starts at a
  vertical neighbour of the control and ends at a horizontal neighbour of the
  target. A qubit that does CNOTs must therefore keep:
  - at least one free vertical side, to act as control; and
  - at least one free horizontal side, to act as target.

  So at most 2 patches in the plane, one on a vertical side and one on a
  horizontal side. That's panel (b), where the CNOT goes through the free sides.

  On top, panel (c):
  - All four bottom sides stay free for CNOTs.
  - The tile directly above the qubit is a "landing" tile surrounded by 4
    patches.
  - A T gate goes up to the landing tile and merges with whichever patch is
    ready. It's still one merge.

  This is a real argument for HBMS: more than 2 patches per qubit is impossible
  in the plane for a qubit that also does CNOTs, and possible on top.

  Two caveats:
  - Spacing. In the drawing the qubits are 4 tiles apart. In square sparse
    they're 2 apart, so neighbouring qubits would share the top patches, giving
    about 2–3 patches per qubit, not 4.
  - Boundary type. A T gate on the X axis must touch the qubit's X-boundary, and
    one on the Z axis its Z-boundary. The cross-layer merge through the landing
    tile has to respect that, so include it when you build the Stim circuit
    (E5).

  - in-plane (B1): 1/4, 1/2, 1 or 2 patches per qubit;
  - on top (P1, P3): 1/4 up to 2–3 patches per qubit.

⏺ About one surface-code patch: roughly 450–512 physical qubits, compared with
  22d² for the 15-to-1 factory you assumed.

  The numbers

  - Gidney, Shutty & Jones 2024: "Cultivation fits inside a surface code patch."
    It ends by growing the T state into a distance-15 patch, about 2·15² ≈ 450
    qubits. Gidney's counting convention, 2(d+1)², gives about 512.
  - IBM Tour de gross (Table 1): their cultivation factory at p = 1e-3 is 454 
    qubits, which matches.

  Cultivation's patch size is fixed by cultivation itself (distance 15), not by
  your data distance d.

  How to count it in your tiles

  The cultivated state must be grown to your data distance d before it's
  consumed. So reserve one full data tile per cultivation patch, the same
  footprint as a data qubit. Gidney 2025 does exactly this: his four
  neighbouring "cultivating" patches are ordinary d = 25 patches.

  1. After a source is used at timestep t, it's ready again at t + 1 + ceil(R/d)

  2. A T gate takes one timestep: one merge with a ready source. If no source is

⏺ The 10 failures are all one bug, in how D5 places factories on the top layer.
  Everything else passed (100 tests).

  What's wrong:
  - The cells above data qubits and above their vertical neighbours are reserved
    so qubits can reach the top layer. That leaves only the even columns of the
    top layer for factories, as one-tile-wide vertical strips.
  - I reused the old shared_k port positions, which put every port in just two
    columns (2 and 6).
  - When the grid has an odd number of data rows (N = 25, 49), the three ports
    in a column need 9 tiles but the column only has 8, while the other even
    columns sit empty.

  Fix:
  - Place ceil(N/k) factories as 4-tile vertical strips spread across all even
    columns, with a free tile between strips so routing can cross.
  - Check afterwards that the top layer is still connected.

  This also makes D5 consistent with D0/D1, which already use ceil(N/k).
